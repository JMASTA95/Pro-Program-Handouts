Supportive care, not a cancer treatment

Nothing in this handout treats cancer. Ozone therapy is not an anticancer therapy, and it is not a substitute for any part of your oncology care. Where it has been measured in cancer patients, it has been measured as supportive care — managing the damage treatment leaves behind. A single session is a stimulus, not a treatment: ozone is consumed within seconds, and what the body receives is a brief messenger pulse that it answers over the following days. Use it only with your oncology team’s knowledge and consent, and let them set the timing around chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

What your body builds afterward

Your own defences get stronger

Your body makes its own protective enzymes that mop up damage. Ozone briefly uses some up, and your cells respond by building more. In one 40-patient course over five weeks, those defences rose between 13.8% and 27.7% — you finish with a bigger reserve than you started with.

Less wear and tear

Doctors measure day-to-day cell damage with a marker called MDA. In the same course it dropped 26.8%. That kind of damage is part of what treatment leaves behind in healthy tissue.

Inflammation settles down

Your body has an on switch and an off switch for inflammation, and ozone nudges the balance toward off. Because it works by building up your antioxidant defences, when you do a session relative to chemotherapy or radiotherapy is a decision for your oncologist, not for you.

Where the evidence sits

The trials below are small, and every one of them measured a complication of cancer treatment rather than the cancer itself. Read them as what they are: evidence that ozone can help with specific, well-defined problems that arise during and after oncology care. Nothing here speaks to tumour response, progression, or survival.

Radiation proctitis

Persistent radiation-induced rectal bleeding after prostate radiotherapy, case series, N=12. Median toxicity grade fell from 3 to 1 and the number of endoscopic treatments from 37 to 4, with no serious adverse events reported.

Breast-cancer-related lymphedema

Single-blind randomized trial, N=68. Rectal ozone added to physical therapy reduced limb volume by 224 mL, against 154 mL for physical therapy alone (p<0.05).

Oral and mucosal care

Ozonated water is the best-evidenced local application: a meta-analysis of 13 RCTs improved periodontal measures, a randomized trial improved erosive oral lichen planus, and a double-blind ICU trial lowered ventilator-associated pneumonia against chlorhexidine.

Safety: the rectal route carries the strongest safety record of any non-IV ozone application. A review of twelve clinical studies plus a case report — 716 patients and more than 46,984 applications — found only mild, transient events such as local irritation or flatulence, on the order of 0.28% at the patient level.
Frequency is the whole program

The adaptation curve has a time constant of roughly 24 to 72 hours. If the next session lands while antioxidant capacity is still elevated, it starts from a higher baseline and the gains ratchet upward over weeks. Space sessions too far apart and each dose resolves completely before the next arrives — repeated starts instead of cumulative adaptation, and you pay the re-priming tax. Five sessions are not five times one session. This is why so-called non-responders are often simply under-dosed on frequency. In supportive care the same logic applies: a session every few weeks is a repeated start, not a program.

What protocols look like

Across 138 randomized trials, protocols cluster around every-other-day to three-times-weekly loading, then weekly to twice-weekly maintenance. A common rectal pattern is daily for three weeks, then one week off. There is always some form of cycling.

Too frequent is possible

Sessions stacked too close, or a dose too high for you, means recovery from the stimulus never completes and stress accumulates instead of adaptation. It is uncommon, but it is patient-dependent and needs monitoring.

Running it well

Set expectations correctly

The cancer-patient evidence is small, supportive-care evidence: a case series and a single-blind trial. Expect help with a specific complication, on the timescale of weeks, and expect nothing about the disease itself.

Track the one problem you are treating — bleeding, limb volume, mouth comfort — alongside sleep and day-to-day energy, and bring those numbers to your oncology appointments.

Talking to your clinician

  1. Clear it with your oncology team first, and tell every clinician treating you that you are using ozone at home.
  2. Ask specifically about timing around chemotherapy and radiotherapy, then agree on a loading block, a maintenance frequency, and a scheduled week off.
  3. Do not use ozone in place of any prescribed treatment, and do not change a prescribed treatment because of how you feel on it.
  4. Never inhale ozone gas. Report new bleeding, infection, or lingering fatigue — those are signals to stop and call, not to push through.
